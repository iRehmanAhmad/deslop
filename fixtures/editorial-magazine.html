On Keeping a Commonplace Book

By Eleanor Vance · 14 October 2026 · Essay No. 41

There is a particular pleasure in copying a sentence out by hand, in feeling the words slow down to the speed of the wrist. The commonplace book — that old, patient technology — asks nothing of its keeper but attention. It does not sync, it does not remind, it does not congratulate. It simply waits, and in waiting it teaches the hand to notice what the eye would have skimmed past on its way to somewhere else entirely.

For three centuries the commonplace book was where readers thought. Scholars kept them, merchants kept them, grandmothers kept them by the kitchen door with the recipes interleaved between the proverbs. A page might hold a line of Seneca above a note about preserving quinces, and the juxtaposition was not a failure of organisation but the whole point: the book became a portrait of a mind, arranged by the order in which the world arrived at it.

We have replaced it, mostly, with the highlight and the bookmark — gestures so cheap they cost no attention at all. The saved article is never reread; the highlighted passage sinks beneath a thousand later highlights. What the commonplace keeper knew, and what the interface designer has forgotten, is that friction is not always the enemy of thought. Sometimes friction is the thought, arriving at its proper pace, refusing to be hurried into a summary.

To copy a sentence is to argue with it quietly. The hand hesitates at the word it does not believe, races over the phrase it loves, and in that small choreography a reader discovers what she actually thinks — which is rarely what she expected to think when she opened the book. The commonplace book is a mirror disguised as a notebook, and its reflections are slower and therefore truer than the ones we catch in passing glass.

Begin, then, with one page and one sentence. Not a system, not a habit stack, not an application with a streak counter — a page, a pen, and ten unhurried minutes. The book will do the rest over years, which is the timescale it was always designed for, and the one our tools have taught us to find unbearable. That unbearableness, examined closely, is the first entry worth copying out.
