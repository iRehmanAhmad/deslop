Gallery IV · North Wing

Instruments of Navigation

Before the satellite, there was the sextant; before the sextant, the cross-staff and a steady hand. The fourteen objects in this room were made to answer one question, asked on every ocean for three hundred years: where are we? Most of them answered it to within thirty miles. The rest of the story was weather, and luck.

Object 4.1

Mariner's astrolabe

Cast brass, diameter 19 cm. Portuguese, c. 1580.

The heavy open ring was made to hang steady in a ship's roll, its alidade sighting the sun at noon. This example was recovered from a wreck off the Scilly Isles in 1967, still legible under four centuries of concretion.

Bequeathed by Captain E. R. Whitmore, 1911. On loan from the reserve collection since 2003.

Object 4.7

Octant in a sharkskin case

Mahogany, ivory scale, silver vernier. London, 1794. Made by J. & W. Cary.

An octant measures a star's height to a fraction of a degree. The case matters as much as the instrument: sharkskin does not rot in salt air, which is why this one survives with its maker's trade card still pasted inside the lid.

Purchased with assistance from the Friends of the Whitmore, 1988.

Object 4.12

A navigator's notebook, kept badly

Paper, ink, rum. HMS Pelican, North Atlantic, 1823–25.

Midshipman Thomas Adey kept his latitudes carefully and his longitudes hopefully. The workings are crossed out, redone, and in one entry simply replaced by a drawing of the ship's cat. The Pelican reached Halifax anyway. So did the cat.

Gift of the Adey family, 1975. Shown open at the cat, by long-standing public demand.
